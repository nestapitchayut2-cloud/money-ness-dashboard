## READING PASSAGE 1 — The Return of the Night Sky
*You should spend about 17 minutes on Questions 1–13.*

For most of human history, anyone who looked up on a clear, moonless night could see several thousand stars with the naked eye. That experience is now rare. According to a widely cited atlas of artificial sky brightness published in 2016, around 80 per cent of the world's population lives under skies affected by artificial light, and more than a third of humanity can no longer see the Milky Way from home. In Europe and North America, the proportion is even higher.

Light pollution takes several forms. 'Skyglow' is the orange or white dome of brightness that hangs over towns and cities; it is produced when light that is emitted upwards, or reflected from roads and buildings, is scattered by particles in the atmosphere. 'Glare' is excessive brightness that causes visual discomfort. It is a particular hazard on the roads for older drivers, whose eyes take longer than those of younger people to recover after exposure to sudden bright light. A third form, 'light trespass', occurs when light falls where it is neither intended nor needed – for example, when a security lamp shines through a neighbour's bedroom window.

Astronomers were the first to raise the alarm. Telescopes are designed to collect faint light, and even a modest increase in background brightness can hide distant galaxies entirely. In 1958, the city of Flagstaff in Arizona, home to the Lowell Observatory, passed what is often described as the world's first outdoor lighting ordinance, restricting certain types of advertising lights. For decades afterwards, however, the issue was regarded as a specialist concern of little relevance to anyone without a telescope.

That view has changed as biologists have documented the effects of artificial light on wildlife. Many migrating birds use the stars to navigate, and brightly lit buildings can draw them off course, sometimes with fatal results. On beaches, newly hatched sea turtles instinctively head towards the brightest horizon, which on an undeveloped coast is the sea, lit by the moon and stars; where hotels and roads line the shore, the hatchlings crawl inland instead. Insects are drawn to lamps in vast numbers, and some researchers believe that artificial light is one of several factors contributing to the decline of insect populations. Even plants respond: trees growing close to streetlights have been observed to keep their leaves later into the autumn than trees in darker spots.

Humans are affected too. Exposure to light at night suppresses the production of melatonin, the hormone that helps regulate sleep, and light that is rich in blue wavelengths is especially effective at doing so. This has created an unexpected problem. Over the past fifteen years, cities around the world have replaced older street lamps with light-emitting diodes (LEDs), which use far less electricity. Yet many early LED installations produced a cold, bluish-white light, and because LEDs are so cheap to run, some authorities simply installed more of them. A study published in 2023, based on more than 50,000 observations by members of the public, concluded that the night sky had been brightening by almost 10 per cent a year between 2011 and 2022 – a far faster rate than satellite measurements had suggested. The researchers explained that satellites are poor at detecting the blue light emitted by many LEDs, and therefore underestimate the true change.

The solutions are, for the most part, neither expensive nor complicated. Fitting shields to light fixtures ensures that light is directed downwards, where it is needed, rather than into the sky. Choosing warmer colours, with less blue light, reduces both skyglow and the effect on sleep. Lights can be dimmed or switched off late at night, and motion sensors can ensure that they come on only when someone is present. A number of towns, parks and islands have gone further and been certified as 'Dark Sky Places' by an international non-profit organisation; some of these communities report that visitors now travel specifically to see the stars. Unlike most forms of pollution, light pollution leaves nothing behind: the moment a lamp is switched off, the problem it caused disappears.

## READING PASSAGE 2 — Learning by Teaching
*You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 14–26.*

**A** 'To teach is to learn twice,' wrote the French essayist Joseph Joubert. Most teachers would agree: many admit that they only truly understood their subject when they first had to explain it to a class. For a long time this was treated as a piece of folk wisdom. Over the past few decades, however, psychologists have tested it systematically, and the benefit that tutors gain from teaching others now has a name: the 'protégé effect'.

**B** One of the most striking findings is that the benefit can begin before any teaching takes place. In a series of experiments, Dr Helen Marsh gave two groups of students the same passage to study. One group was told to expect a test; the other was told that they would later have to teach the material to another student. In fact, neither group taught anyone – both simply took the test. Yet the students who had expected to teach recalled more of the passage and organised their answers more logically. Marsh argues that the expectation of teaching changes how people study: they look for the main ideas and the connections between them, rather than trying to memorise isolated facts.

**C** Professor Arun Patel believes that the main benefit comes at a later stage – when the explanation is actually given. In his view, what matters is not explaining as such but retrieval: being forced to recall information from memory. In one study, he asked students to teach a topic to a video camera, either with their notes in front of them or without. Those who taught without notes performed considerably better on a test a week later. Those who could read from their notes gained little more than students who had simply reread the material. 'Explaining is useful,' Patel says, 'mostly because it makes you dig the information out of your own head.'

**D** A third line of research focuses on motivation. Dr Lena Fischer and her colleagues developed 'teachable agents' – simple computer characters that students instruct and that then take quizzes based on what they have been taught. Fischer found that students spent more time preparing when they were teaching an agent than when they were studying for their own test. Many said they felt responsible for the character and did not want it to fail. The effect was strongest among lower-achieving students, who often lacked the motivation to study for themselves but would make an effort on behalf of someone else.

**E** None of this means that learning by teaching is without drawbacks. Preparing to teach takes considerably longer than ordinary study, and that time must come from somewhere. Tutors who have not fully understood a topic may pass their own misconceptions on to the people they teach, and the benefit to the tutor depends on having at least a basic grasp of the material in the first place. Marsh herself cautions that peer teaching should be seen as an addition to expert instruction, not a replacement for it.

**F** For individual learners, the simplest application is to explain a concept aloud, in plain language, to an imaginary listener, and to notice where the explanation breaks down; these gaps show what still needs to be studied. Institutions can use the idea too. Several universities run schemes in which second-year students tutor first-year students in difficult introductory courses. Because the tutors are students themselves, such schemes cost relatively little to run, and both groups appear to benefit.

## READING PASSAGE 3 — The Box That Shrank the World
*You should spend about 23 minutes on Questions 27–40.*

In April 1956, a converted oil tanker called the Ideal-X left Newark, New Jersey, for Houston, Texas, carrying 58 metal boxes on its deck. Few people at the time regarded the voyage as historic. Yet within a few decades the shipping container would transform not only the transport industry but the geography of the world economy.

Before containerisation, most cargo was 'break-bulk': sacks, barrels and crates loaded and unloaded one at a time by large gangs of dockworkers. A ship could spend as long tied up in port as it did at sea, and theft from the docks was common. As a result, the cost of moving goods was dominated not by the ocean voyage but by handling at either end. Malcom McLean, the trucking entrepreneur behind the Ideal-X, later estimated that loading loose cargo cost $5.86 per ton, compared with about 16 cents per ton for his containers.

It would be a mistake, however, to think that the box itself was the breakthrough. Metal containers had been used on railways and ships for decades before 1956. What McLean understood – and what many of his competitors were slow to grasp – was that a container is only valuable as part of a system in which ships, cranes, trucks and trains are all designed around the same unit. A box that cannot be lifted straight from a ship onto a lorry saves very little.

Creating such a system required standardisation, and that took years of argument. Each shipping line initially used its own sizes, and companies that had already invested heavily in their own designs had little reason to abandon them. Through the 1960s, committees of the International Organization for Standardization negotiated dimensions, strength requirements and the fittings at each corner by which boxes are lifted and locked together. The outcome was the familiar 20-foot and 40-foot containers, and the industry still measures capacity in 'twenty-foot equivalent units', or TEU. The large-scale use of containers to supply American forces during the Vietnam War helped to convince sceptics that the system was reliable.

The consequences for ports and their workers were profound. Container terminals need vast areas of flat land beside deep water, rather than the narrow piers of old city centres. In New York, traffic moved from the Manhattan and Brooklyn waterfronts to new facilities in New Jersey; in Britain, the historic London docks closed one after another while the small port of Felixstowe grew into the country's busiest. The number of dockworkers fell sharply wherever containers arrived. Histories of the container tend to treat these losses as a footnote to a story of progress, but for the communities that had been built around the docks, they were the main story.

The wider economic effects are harder to measure. Cheap and predictable transport made it practical for manufacturers to divide production between countries – making components in one place, assembling them in another and selling the finished product somewhere else. One econometric study concluded that containerisation did more to increase trade between developed countries over a twenty-year period than all the trade agreements of the same era. Other economists point out that it is difficult to separate the effect of the container from that of falling tariffs, cheaper fuel and computerised logistics, and they are right to be cautious: these changes happened together and reinforced one another.

Meanwhile, ships have grown steadily larger. The biggest vessels now carry more than 24,000 TEU, and each increase in size has lowered the cost of carrying a single box. But only a limited number of ports can handle such ships, and when the Ever Given became stuck in the Suez Canal in 2021, blocking traffic for six days, the disruption showed how vulnerable the system had become. It seems likely that the race towards ever-larger vessels will slow – not because engineers are unable to build bigger ships, but because ports and canals cannot accommodate them economically.
